## Religion, Politics, and Cognitive Warfare: Information, Interpretation, Conspiracy, and the Struggle for Reality

<p style="text-align:center; margin: 2rem 0;"><a class="btn btn-primary btn-lg rounded-pill" href="https://forms.gle/JptSqcCk19An5T3h7" style="display:inline-block; background:#B42318; color:#ffffff; font-weight:700; padding:1rem 2rem; border-radius:999px; text-decoration:none; font-size:1.25rem; line-height:1.2; box-shadow:0 8px 18px rgba(180,35,24,0.25);">Register Now</a></p>

### Details
- **When:** October 1-3, 2026
- **Where:** Online
- **Registration:** Now open
- **Sponsored by:** The Journal for Cultural and Religious Theory (JCRT) in collaboration with the University of Denver

This conference investigates how beliefs, paranoia, and conspiratorial modes of knowing shape a contemporary cognitive battlespace in which actors struggle to define truth, authority, and reality itself. Drawing on Michel Foucault’s *Lectures on the Will to Know* and Julia Kristeva’s *This Incredible Need to Believe* (2024), the event focuses on practices of selective fact use and “truth-selecting” that construct alternative epistemic orders while claiming privileged access to what is “really” going on.

Rather than treating conspiracy thinking as merely political or psychological, the conference foregrounds its religious dimensions: faith in hidden powers, moral dualism, apocalyptic expectation, and hermeneutical struggle over revelatory access to the real. Conspiracy cultures routinely reproduce theological structures of knowing—visions of salvation and corruption, truth and deception, initiation and blindness. At the same time, new forms of “cognitive warfare” reframe these religious energies within state and platform attempts to govern perception, attention, and trust. Artificial intelligence, algorithmic media, and strategic information campaigns do not only deliver messages; they create digital ecologies in which suspicion becomes faith-like and revelation is continual.

The conference organizers welcome contributions from scholars of religious studies, philosophy, communication, sociology, security studies, psychology, media, and related fields. We also encourage work that takes a reflective and analytical approach to both general and specific topics with international appeal or focus. Submissions should engage religion as a dynamic force—conceptually, historically, or materially—within the cognitive battlespaces of our time.

### Keynote Address: Militarizing the Mind: Cognitive Warfare from the Cold War to the Dark Web

### [Jason Ānanda Josephson Storm](https://hub.williams.edu/religion/faculty/jason-josephson/)

As I currently conceive it, the talk will open with three unsettling contemporary snapshots: a dark web occult repository of conspiracy theory screeds and “psychic mind-control” manuals; a NATO tactical statement declaring the human mind the “sixth domain” of warfare; and the familiar structure of a TikTok algorithmic rabbit hole. Together, these prismatic scenes suggest shared conceptions of the mind and a common repertoire of techniques for controlling perception, attention, and even thought itself.

The talk will trace the emergence of these conceptions of the mind to a Cold War military industrial complex and the convergence of cybernetics, psychic research, propaganda studies, and Soviet theories of reflexive control. From these entanglements emerged many of the psychological models and informational logics that continue to govern contemporary life. Engaging Han Byung-Chul’s “psychopolitics,” Bernard Stiegler’s account of the alienation of memory, N. Katherine Hayles’ account of virtual bodies, and Friedrich Kittler’s insight that civilian media infrastructures are basically sedimented military technologies, I will examine how modern systems of influence increasingly operate through feedback loops designed to capture attention, circumvent deliberation, and exploit fractures in epistemic authority and trust.

*[Jason Ānanda Josephson Storm](https://religion.williams.edu/faculty/jason-josephson/) is the Francis Christopher Oakley Third Century Professor of Religion, Chair of Science & Technology Studies at Williams College and author of* [The Genealogy of Genealogy: Nietzsche, Foucault, and the Coils of Critical History](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/G/bo265446241.html) *and* [Metamodernism: The Future of Theory](https://amzn.to/36LNa2q).

<details class="jcrt-sched__abstract"><summary>Eschatology as Cognitive Warfare: How Apocalyptic Theology Reshapes American Christian Nationalism&#x27;s Truth Regime</summary>
<div>
<p>This paper proposes that contemporary American Christian Nationalism operates less as political theology and more as a cognitive warfare apparatus — a system in which apocalyptic eschatology is weaponized to restructure the epistemic architecture of political reality itself. Drawing on Foucault&#x27;s Lectures on the Will to Know (2013) and Hofstadter&#x27;s (1964) paranoid hermeneutics, I trace how Christian nationalist movements construct an alternative &quot;truth regime&quot; through selective scriptural citation, conspiratorial narratives of decline, and eschatological urgency recasting democratic deliberation as zero-sum cosmic struggle. Kristeva&#x27;s (2009) &quot;pre-religious need to believe&quot; explains why apocalyptic narratives retain cognitive traction even as formal religious participation declines.</p>
<p>The paper advances a three-dimensional framework of structural religious alienation, integrating the sociology of religion with critical theory to conceptualize how theological tropes are translated into assertions of sovereign power. First, at the level of faith ontology, salvation anxiety degenerates into a thirst for secular domination (Weber, 1946) — the theological category of &quot;redemption&quot; is repurposed as a mandate for institutional capture. Second, at the level of organizational form, the Durkheimian sacred born of collective effervescence corrodes into sectarian fanaticism (Durkheim, 1912/1995), transforming religious communities into disciplined political cadres. Third, at the level of discursive logic, theological revelation is reduced to what Marcuse (1964) termed &quot;one-dimensional&quot; language — a closed semantic system in which dissenting interpretations are preemptively coded as demonic deception. Drawing on Barkun&#x27;s (2003) concept of stigmatized knowledge, I argue that apocalyptic eschatology functions as an epistemic gatekeeping mechanism: it claims privileged revelatory access to hidden realities while simultaneously delegitimizing secular knowledge production as epistemically corrupt — performing the dual cognitive warfare operations of truth-claiming and truth-invalidating in a single theological gesture.</p>
<p>Empirically, I examine the Seven Mountain Mandate and Project 2025 as paradigmatic cases of this cognitive warfare strategy. The Seven Mountain Mandate — the theopolitical doctrine commanding Christian seizure of seven cultural domains (government, education, media, arts, business, family, and religion) — functions as a totalizing epistemic blueprint that recodes every institution as a battlefield in the war for truth. Project 2025 operationalizes this blueprint through personnel replacement, regulatory restructuring, and the weaponization of unitary executive theory, all within an apocalyptic temporality that generates theological justifications for political action beyond procedural democratic constraints.</p>
<p>The paper contributes to the conference&#x27;s inquiry into how paranoid styles and truth-selecting practices construct alternative epistemic orders by demonstrating that Christian nationalist eschatology performs precisely the function of a cognitive warfare doctrine: it provides a hermeneutics of suspicion that delegitimizes secular knowledge institutions, a moral dualism that transforms political opponents into cosmic enemies, and an apocalyptic expectation that normalizes theologically sanctioned departures from procedural democratic norms as a form of spiritual warfare. Methodologically, the paper introduces a comparative dimension rarely deployed in American-focused Christian nationalism scholarship. Juxtaposing the U.S. case — in which eschatological truth claims are embedded in state institutional design — with the Chinese constitutional model of church–state boundary regulation, the analysis isolates the institutional mechanisms through which apocalyptic theology crosses from private belief to public cognitive warfare infrastructure.</p>

<details class="jcrt-sched__abstract"><summary>Evangelical Discernment in the Age of AI</summary>
<div>
<p>As evangelicals have risen to prominence in American politics, belief, conspiracy, and misinformation have also become increasingly enmeshed. Media technologies such as radio, film, and the written word each play key roles in buttressing the political and religious activities of these actors, who craft complex networks of religious authority, knowledge production, and claims to truth. These systems now collide with digital infrastructures, and the recent proliferation of artificial intelligence (AI) brings the ethical and political stakes of evangelical truth-seeking into sharp relief. Few evangelical media objects are as startling as those touched by AI, from Christian Zionist documentaries to Donald Trump&#x27;s infamous self-portrayal as Christ.</p>
<p>This paper explores the ways in which evangelicals evaluate the efficacy and appropriate uses of AI. Drawing from several months of online and in-person ethnographic fieldwork, I focus on the perspectives of the Christian tech professionals working to integrate AI into congregational spaces, spiritual practice, and missionary work. I show that my interlocutors do not embrace these technologies wholesale but actively negotiate their religious veracity through a deliberate process of revelatory, Spirit-driven discernment. In doing so, these developers seriously reckon with the ways AI could be co-opted or abused while also expressing deep excitement for how algorithms, text-to-image generators, and other AI tools can be leveraged “for the Kingdom.”</p>
<p>Throughout the paper, I leverage ethnographic vignettes and media objects to show the dynamic—and at times paradoxical—ways my interlocutors approach their calling as AI evangelists. I explain how these believers work to build trust in AI through a discursive process that remains unsettled while they search for what God reveals to them about the machine. As they invent, design, and create their way towards God’s plan for artificial intelligence, the boundaries between propriety, ingenuity, and sacrilege remain ever in flux. The social relationships emerging between human, God, and machine in this process hold sweeping implications for the ways we might approach evangelical epistemic politics as a subject of cultural and religious inquiry.</p>
</div>

<details class="jcrt-sched__abstract"><summary>In The World But Not of it: Evangelical Epistemologies, Alternative Realities, and the Search for the Real</summary>
<div>
<p>The boundary between this world and the next has always been porous among those inheritors of American Evangelicalism (Boyer, 1992). From a predilection for interpreting global events with an eye toward an already/not yet eschaton; to founding think-tanks, educational institutions, and media empires that would far outlive their founders (Fitzgerald, 2017). From asserting the inerrancy and historicity of the Bible; to ascending Mount Everest to wage spiritual warfare, successfully unseating the “queen of heaven” (Ladner, 2026). Some might scandalize this progeny as out of touch, hypocritical, weird, “other.” Some may highlight the anxieties of modernity and the subsequent response to these extraneous conditions (Sutton, 2017).</p>
<p>The aim of this paper is to, as Susan Harding implores to “interrogate representation itself” so that “we may ask how “fundamentalism” was invented, who speaks it, and what are the categories, assumptions, and trajectories implicit in its narrative representations” (1991). As such, this paper will consider how epistemologies among American Evangelicals have supplanted critical reason in exchange for the supranatural; and in doing so manufacture alternative realities. I will argue that the existence of these alternatives is not the result of a dismissal of what is real - rather, it is the consequential search for it - in the midst of an ever alienating world. I will go on to demonstrate how these realities are liable to nationalist myths, exploitation, and political manipulation. Finally, I will explore how these epistemologies depend upon an authority that is both out of reach but easily accessible; which, I argue, makes it especially malleable by propagandists and authoritarians in their pursuit of power. As I conclude I will examine the trajectories that are opened up as we consider the reality of these alternatives and their consequences on our shared democratic nation.</p>

<details class="jcrt-sched__abstract"><summary>Sanad-less Knowledge: AI as an Epistemically Untrustworthy Mediator of Religious Authority in the Age of Cognitive Warfare</summary>
<div>
<p>The Escalation of generative AI becomes a source of religious guidance, it fundamentally changes the way believers can access and validate religious knowledge. In the current era Complex theological issues can be answered instantly by platforms like ChatGPT, Quran GPT, Hadith GPT and other AI-powered fatwa chatbots, now offer instantaneous answers to complex theological questions, apart from trained scholars who have established verifiable chains of transmission (sanad) and whose answers are morally accountable. This change takes place in a digital environment that is defined by misinformation, polarization and systematic bias, and where misinformation about religious communities is perpetuated by machine learning models trained with biased data. In the current era of cognitive warfare, the research aim is to explore: what role can AI play in religious knowledge mediation? This research derives from the Islamic epistemological tradition that sees the authenticity of knowledge as being rooted in the chain of transmission (sanad), methodological criticism (naqd) and scholarly consensus, and thus distinguishes between the two different types of trust: epistemic trust suitable for the limited cognitive functions of AI and moral trust or religious trust which are the ones that AI lacks. The approach used is a qualitative synthesis of recent empirical research and theoretical work on the nexus of artificial intelligence, religious epistemology and cognitive warfare. The research comprises a keen analysis of AI generated religious content that mimics clerics&#x27; style of speech and conjures up sacred symbols, comparative overview of the epistemological trust of AI responses to religious questions with institutional fatwas, and the critical examination of works of scholarship about algorithmic authority, epistemological trust, and the transformation of religious authority in digital environments. This study has four implications. First, it shows that AI is not only the spreader of incorrect religious information, but it also changes how religious truth is identified, which is done away with technical accuracy and in favour of moral responsibility and the creation of what religious scholars describe as not authentic. Second, it shows how a new type of religious power is emerging: “algorithmic authority” and “infrastructure authority,” that supersede the traditional religious power of scholars with their quick and effortless religious answers which they perceive as objective knowledge. Third, it explains the transition from a system of epistemology to an epistemology of systems, in which the traditional hierarchy of authority has been undermined and computational legitimacy has become more significant. Fourth, and most importantly, the paper bridges epistemic crisis and cognitive warfare, wherein AI- generated religious content is increasingly being used as a weapon to undermine people&#x27;s trust in society, create false authority, shape belief systems, and exploit religious divisions. The core theme of the research to revitalize digital literacy, algorithm transparency, community-driven fact checking and religiously informed ethics of technology to curb</p>
<p>epistemic harm and reaffirm the importance of human scholarship in the transmission of religious knowledge.</p>

<details class="jcrt-sched__abstract"><summary>AI Sacralization Tendencies in Zhihu User-Generated Content</summary>
<div>
<p>This paper presents findings from a micro-ethnographic discourse analysis of AI-related content on Zhihu (知乎), China’s leading intellectual Q&amp;A platform. Drawing on a purposively sampled corpus of 54 questions, answers, comments, and articles posted between 2023 and 2025, the study applies Razmetaeva’s (2023) eight-category typology of AI sacralization to examine how users employ religious and sacred vocabulary when discussing AI.</p>
<p>The analysis identifies seven of the eight sacralization tendencies, with AI most frequently framed through quasi-divine attributes and as a source of authority. Users spontaneously employ concepts such as faith, theocracy, and spiritual intelligence, suggesting that sacralizing interpretations of AI emerge within the discourse itself rather than being imposed by the researcher. At the same time, the absence of the “sacrifice of privacy” category highlights the limits of applying Western-derived frameworks across different cultural contexts.</p>
<p>The findings also reveal a tension in how AI is imagined: users describe it both as a radically non-human and impartial authority and as an intimate confidant capable of listening, comforting, and offering guidance. By tracing how religious language is mobilized around AI on a major Chinese platform, the paper explores emerging relationships between artificial intelligence, authority and belief.</p>

<details class="jcrt-sched__abstract"><summary>US American Christian Imaginaries: The Aestheticization of Faith and Political Spectacle in AI-Generated Media</summary>
<div>
<p>In April 2026, President Donald Trump posted an AI-generated image of himself, which has become somewhat standard fare for his social media presence. However, this particular image sparked backlash with his depiction as Jesus performing a healing miracle. In the proliferation of AI-generated media in social media spaces, representations of Jesus take on numerous forms across platforms and modes, from images to chatbots and beyond. This paper seeks to critically examine the aesthetic characteristics of AI-generated images featuring Jesus or a Christ-like figure, the mobilization of Christian imaginaries within US American conservative cultural productions, and the potential ramifications of these religiously-loaded ideological replications. <div>
<p>This paper examines cinema as a central site in the formation of contemporary “truth-selecting” practices, arguing that Hollywood film has long functioned as a training ground for the paranoid and revelatory modes of knowing that now characterize the cognitive battlespace. Drawing on Michel Foucault’s account of the “will to know” and Julia Kristeva’s notion of the “need to believe,” I argue that cinematic spectatorship operates as a form of secularized hermeneutics in which viewers are invited to decode reality as if it were structured by hidden forces. Hollywood cinema—particularly in genres such as the conspiracy thriller and the “puzzle film”—repeatedly stages a world in which appearances deceive and truth must be excavated through suspicion. Films such as The Matrix (1999), Enemy of the State (1998), Minority Report (2002), and Inception (2010) construct narrative universes governed by invisible systems of control, where protagonists gain privileged access to a concealed layer of reality. It also draws on Laura Mulvey’s account of visual pleasure and spectatorship to argue that cinematic looking is never neutral but structured by desire, projection, and interpretive framing. Together, these perspectives help explain how cinema trains viewers to see beyond the surface, cultivating a hermeneutics of suspicion that easily extends into conspiratorial modes of world-making. This cinematic trajectory culminates in a digital landscape where the traditional boundaries of the frame have dissolved into the expanded battlespace of cognitive warfare. With the rise of deepfakes and AI-generated video, the indexical link between image and reality—already destabilized in film theory—becomes increasingly untenable. If contemporary subjects inhabit a world increasingly experienced as a film to be decoded, then the task of criticism is not simply to debunk false images but to understand the enduring appeal of revelation itself. By situating Hollywood cinema within these religious and epistemic dynamics, this paper argues that the struggle for reality today is inseparable from the cinematic forms through which it is imagined and lived.</p>

<div>
<p>Contemporary polycrisis (Rakowski et al., 2025, p. 176) displays what Beck (1992, pp. 75–76) already predicted in his theory of his “risk society”: its stochastically distributed risks are increasingly invisible, while the consequences of entangled crises degrade social and political systems, become increasingly difficult to ignore. At the same time, decisive threats can no longer be witnessed directly but become only accessible through competing scientific and counter-scientific interpretations. This condition intensifies what Berger et al. (1974, p. 82) describe as modernity’s deepening metaphysical “homelessness”: the loss of a sense of ““being at home” in society, with oneself and, ultimately, in the universe”, accompanied by a longing for such a condition. Individualisation has loosened the boundaries and obligations once imposed by society, expanding the range of possible identities and beliefs. Yet the erosion of these inherited structures also weakens the sources of certainty and belonging they once provided. This paper argues that these circumstances create favourable conditions for the appeal of conspiratorial meaning systems as attempts to reconstruct (at least in part) a metaphysical home. QAnon is taken as an ideal-typical case of how quasi-religious movements perform religious work and is conceptualised as a hyperreal religion adapted to late-modern markets of meaning. These movements locate evil in hidden forces operating outside the imagined true community (Barkun, 2013, pp. 3–4). Berger’s sociology of religion (Berger, 1967, 1984, 2008) and Berger and Luckmann’s theory of the social construction of reality (1966, p. 180) is then discussed as an instrumentarium for analysing how these alternative structures of relevance (Barkun, 2015) are adopted and how a knowledge system that (might) appear implausible to outsiders becomes subjectively real. As consumer markets individualise commodities, these movements individualise (an implicit promise of) salvation through combinations of political suspicion and ‘spiritual awakening’. Ward and Voas (2011, p. 104) concept of ‘conspirituality’ captures this configuration particularly well: building on Barkun (2013), they describe it as the fusion of conspiracy belief with an anticipated transformation of consciousness – while delivering the immediate and this-worldly promise of ‘awakening’ and liberation from manipulation. A quasi-soteriological structure illustrates QAnon’s relation to religion. While for example the collective interpretation of revelatory texts (such as ‘Q drops’) is structurally analogous to lay exegesis, at the same time QAnon lacks the important factors commonly associated with religious movements (Foertsch et al., 2024, pp. 75–76). The resistance to established classifications suggests its conceptualisation as a hyperreal religion: unlike earlier new religious movements, it is not tethered to the reality principle (Baudrillard, 2019, p. 13) in the same way, but operates through a fluid assemblage of signs and political interpretations. Finally, the distinction between orthodoxy and heterodoxy helps locate these knowledge systems within a contested field, but does not determine their epistemological status or capacity for revision (Bourdieu, 2010a, p. 473, 2010b, p. 164). It must therefore be supplemented by epistemic revisability as a second criterion, which distinguishes a knowledge system’s doxic status from its openness to correction.</p>

<p>Conspiracy thinking, this paper argues, is best understood not as a surplus of suspicion but as a deficit of perspective—an epistemology of closure. <details class="jcrt-sched__abstract"><summary>The Threshold Not Even God Can Cross: Perceived Determinacy and The Reality of Underdetermination.</summary>
<div>
<p>This paper asks why deeply held religious, ideological, and conspiratorial beliefs become publicly authoritative despite persistent interpretive disagreement. I argue that contemporary cognitive warfare exploits the gap between perceived determinacy and the reality of interpretive underdetermination. Drawing on Michel Foucault’s account of truth production, Julia Kristeva’s analysis of the human need to believe, and contemporary hermeneutical theory, I show that private certainty and public legitimacy are fundamentally different epistemic conditions. To explain this distinction, I introduce the concept of the Public Determinacy Threshold (PDT), a criterion for evaluating when competing interpretations can legitimately ground publicly coercive authority. Human beings do not encounter reality directly but through interpretive frameworks mediated by language, culture, memory, institutions, and tradition. Consequently, the same phenomenon can generate multiple internally coherent interpretations, producing a condition of persistent hermeneutical underdetermination. At the same time, psychological pressures associated with uncertainty encourage the formation of internal stabilizing frameworks that transform ambiguity into perceived certainty. This dynamic helps explain the enduring power of religious revelation, conspiracy narratives, ideological commitments, and other systems of meaning. Such frameworks often provide cognitive and existential stabilization by organizing experience into coherent realities that appear self-evident from within the interpretive community. Cognitive warfare exploits this condition by contesting not merely facts but the frameworks through which facts acquire meaning, authority, and legitimacy. The Public Determinacy Threshold provides a criterion for determining when competing interpretations can legitimately ground publicly coercive authority. It defines the minimum condition under which such claims can be publicly evaluated without requiring prior acceptance of the contested interpretive framework. While private certainty may be psychologically compelling and existentially meaningful, claims that remain interpretively underdetermined cannot automatically generate publicly legitimate authority. The problem confronting pluralistic societies is therefore not belief itself, but the conversion of privately justified certainty into publicly coercive authority under conditions of persistent underdetermination. This paper concludes that the central challenge of contemporary cognitive warfare is not the existence of competing realities but the attempt to transform perceived determinacy into socially binding truth. Cognitive warfare exploits the tension between perceived determinacy and the reality of underdetermination. Democratic legitimacy therefore depends upon maintaining standards of public justification capable of distinguishing private conviction from publicly authoritative claims. The threshold separating those domains is one that no claim, including claims of revelation, can cross merely by virtue of certainty alone.</p>

<details class="jcrt-sched__abstract"><summary>Counterfeit Gnosis: Conspiracy Culture and the Need for Formation</summary>
<div>
<p>The contemporary struggle over reality is routinely diagnosed as an information problem. This paper argues that it is more fundamentally a problem of formation -- the initiatory and disciplinary practices through which human beings are shaped into subjects capable of bearing interpretive responsibility rather than being shaped by the epistemic environments they inhabit. Interpretation is a formed capacity: formation is the mechanism, interpretation its output. Every epistemic order produces not only knowledge but knowers (Foucault); when the mediating structures that once formed interpreters weaken and collapse, the constitutive need to believe (Kristeva) is not eliminated but available for capture by whatever environment most efficiently supplies the experience of epistemic location -- a sense of where one stands.</p>

Initiatory and esoteric traditions, among others, can be read not for their doctrine but as formation systems: disciplines by which the reparative may be cultivated and governed.</p>

<div>
<p>This paper examines philosemitism as a form of cognitive and affective governance that regulates the conditions under which Jewishness becomes culturally intelligible, emotionally legible, and socially acceptable within nineteenth-century American literary discourse. Focusing on Sidney Luska’s Jewish novels—As It Was Written (1885), Mrs. Peixada (1886), and The Yoke of the Thorah (1887)—the paper argues that philosemitic admiration operates not simply as benevolent feeling or sympathetic representation, but as a regulatory interpretive structure that organizes visibility, emotional value, and epistemic authority. Written by Henry Harland under the Jewish pseudonym “Sidney Luska,” these novels occupy a revealing position in the formation of American Jewish representation: Jewishness becomes narratively central while remaining mediated through non-Jewish interpretive authority. Rather than reading philosemitism as merely the inverse of antisemitism, the paper theorizes it as a mode of “truth-selection” that stabilizes Jewish difference into emotionally recognizable and aesthetically manageable forms. Luska’s fiction repeatedly renders Jewishness legible through sentimental suffering, aesthetic refinement, artistic sensitivity, moral seriousness, and inherited emotional depth. Such representations appear inclusive and sympathetic, yet they simultaneously regulate the terms on which Jewish identity may enter public visibility. Drawing on affect theory, Foucauldian approaches to discourse and epistemic authority, and theories of religious hermeneutics, the paper situates philosemitism within broader histories of mediated knowledge and interpretive governance. The analysis demonstrates how Luska’s fiction transforms Jewishness into an object of admiration precisely through selective acts of interpretation that convert cultural difference into emotionally consumable forms. Jewish women in particular function as affective mediators through whom Jewishness becomes morally legible and aesthetically reassuring, while forms of Jewish identity associated with political agency, collective demand, social conflict, or resistant self-definition remain displaced, softened, or excluded altogether. The paper further argues that philosemitism operates as a cognitive structure that produces recognizable truths about Jewishness while concealing the asymmetries of interpretive power through which such truths are constructed. Admiration itself becomes a mechanism of governance: a way of organizing minority visibility through emotional regulation, sentimental framing, and aesthetic containment. In this framework, Jewishness is not erased but carefully curated, rendered acceptable insofar as it conforms to established affective expectations within dominant Anglo-American literary culture. Situating Luska’s novels within contemporary discussions of epistemic struggle, mediated reality, and cognitive governance, the paper suggests that philosemitic discourse anticipates modern forms of symbolic management in which emotional identification and interpretive control operate simultaneously. By approaching philosemitism as a cognitive and affective regime rather than merely a moral disposition, the paper contributes to broader discussions concerning religion, interpretation, conspiracy, and the politics of social reality. <details class="jcrt-sched__abstract"><summary>From Ritual Murder to Political Paranoia: Blood Libel as a Conspiratorial Hermeneutic</summary>
<div>
<p>This paper examines how the theological imaginary of blood libel is rearticulated as a conspiratorial political hermeneutic within ostensibly secular discourse. Emerging from medieval Christian theology, the blood libel cast alleged violence against non-Jews - paradigmatically, though not exclusively, the ritual murder of children - as evidence of concealed Jewish intention and collective guilt. It also furnished a framework through which suffering was interpreted as revealing the supposedly hidden nature and purposes of Jews as a collective. The paper traces the selective adaptation of this framework beyond its original doctrinal setting, particularly the movement from visible harm to concealed agency, malign collective purpose and transhistorical guilt. Blood libel is approached not simply as a recurring accusation, but as a framework governing how political events are made intelligible, where agency is located and what counts as evidence. Its conspiratorial character lies in the presumption of a hidden, coordinated and malign collective actor. Theological motifs become politically operative when ritual intention is reworked as concealed state, institutional or geopolitical purpose; collective theological guilt becomes political culpability; and theological constructions of concealed Jewish agency inform claims about covert political power. Particular political events or harms may then be interpreted not as contingent outcomes attributable to identifiable actors, but as revelations of an enduring collective intention produced through the conflation of Jews, Israelis and Zionists. Within this framework, contrary evidence can be incorporated as further proof of influence, deception or concealment.</p>
<p>Combining historical genealogy with close interpretive analysis of selected contemporary political discourse, the paper asks when criticism of religion, Zionism or Israeli state policy becomes structured by inherited blood-libel hermeneutics. The severity or systemic character of an allegation is not itself diagnostic: accounts of civilian death, bodily harm or patterned violence may form part of evidence-based arguments about individual, institutional or structural responsibility. The distinction depends instead on the symbolic framing of the claim, its attribution of agency and the extent to which the attribution of responsibility remains open to evidentiary qualification or revision. The paper identifies a cumulative conjunction of features: the construction and conflation of Jews, Israelis and Zionists as a unified and concealed collective agent; the interpretation of non-Jewish suffering as evidence of a purpose represented as essentially and transhistorically Jewish; the activation of historically recognisable motifs of secret Jewish violence against non-Jews; and resistance to evidentiary qualification or disconfirmation. Historically recognisable motifs establish the connection to blood libel, collective attribution and essentialisation establish its antisemitic structure, and the presumption of hidden agency, combined with resistance to revision, reveals its conspiratorial epistemology. <div>
<p>This paper begins from a question that conspiracy theory forces into the open: what makes it possible for people to inhabit a shared reality together, and how does that possibility collapse? <p>This study examines the intertwining of homophobic and antisemitic conspiracy theories on two Chinese Maoist forums, Wuyouzhixiang and Kunlunce. Instead of pathologizing these texts through the conventional anti-Marxist lens (e.g. Schumpeter 1942; Popper 1945), I turn to Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick’s queer “reparative reading” (1997) to empathize with these individuals’ socio-economic displacements in a neo-liberal society, seeking the possibility of healing and love instead of reinforcing the paranoid infrastructure against radical thoughts. To achieve this goal, I draw upon Julia Kristeva (2024) and her concept of the adolescence as “a syndrome of ideality.” Because these Marxists’ repetitive chanting of Marxist-Maoist quotes constitutes performativity, constructing an exclusive circle where their Maoist belief has not fallen, I argue that this performativity is embedded with a form of quasi-religiosity that is better understood as Kristeva’s adolescent ideality, a necessity for human beings to believe without reference to religion. Disillusioned by China’s marketized economic ascent, globalized financial abstraction, and enlarging wealth gap, their ontological security has been severely fractured. The loss of their idealized historical “Great Other”—the pure Maoist era—hurls them into what Kristeva describes as the “ruins of paradise,” wherein the frustrated drive for ideality regresses into a paranoid search for persecutory scapegoats, most saliently articulated through antisemitic conspiracy theories popularized by texts like Currency Wars. Furthermore, I engage with Judith Butler’s critique of homonationalism and her thesis on precarity. The Maoist anxiety towards queer culture and their antisemitism deny their alienation and misrecognize the genuine precarity of life under global capitalism. Yet, to repair this paranoid infrastructure and achieve the “promise of politics” as advocated by Hannah Arendt, this paper critiques the long-established academic tendency to simply dismiss Marxism as either anti-religious or religious, which constitutes the paranoid infrastructure that breeds conspiracy theory as well.</p>
